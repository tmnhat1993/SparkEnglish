Why Class Size Matters

The Smallest Detail With the Biggest Effect

Class size decides how much of a lesson actually reaches your child. At Spark, that number is 4–6 students — never more.

  1. Home
  2. /About
  3. /Why Class Size Matters
In short

Why 4–6 students, not 20–30

  • Large classes give each child roughly 2–3 individual feedback moments per lesson.
  • Spark's small classes give each child 20–25 feedback moments in the same 45 minutes.
  • More feedback means mistakes get corrected before they become habits.

The Problem With Large Classes

In a class of 20–30, even an excellent teacher can only reach each student for a few seconds at a time. Most instruction has to be aimed at the "average" student — too slow for some, too fast for others — and a child who is quietly stuck often stays that way for weeks before anyone notices.

4–6

students per Spark class — small enough that every child is heard, corrected and encouraged in every single lesson.

What Changes When Class Size Shrinks

What parents notice within a few weeks

  • Stronger participation. There's nowhere to stay silent in a group of 4–6 — quiet students get gently drawn in.
  • Faster correction. Mistakes are fixed the moment they happen, before they become habits.
  • Real pacing. Lessons move at a pace that fits the students actually in the room.
  • Visible confidence. Children who rarely spoke up in large classes start volunteering answers.

Common Questions

FAQs About Class Size

Why does Spark cap classes at 4–6 students?

At this size, a teacher can hear every child read, see every child's writing, and correct each student's specific mistake in every lesson. Beyond about 6 students, meaningful individual feedback drops off sharply.

Do you offer one-to-one private lessons?

Private lessons are available based on teacher evaluation and availability. Most students progress fastest in a small group, where peer discussion and modeling add another layer of learning alongside individual feedback.

Will my shy child get lost in a group, even a small one?

It's the opposite of what usually happens in large classes. In a group of 4–6, quieter students are gently drawn into discussion and get noticed immediately if they're stuck — there's nowhere to stay invisible, in a good way.
